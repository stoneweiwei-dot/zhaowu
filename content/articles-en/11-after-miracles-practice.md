# How to Practice After the Miracles Are Gone

People sometimes begin spiritual practice hoping for certainty: Am I special? Do the gods or Buddhas favor me? Can I see what others cannot? Yet if practice becomes collecting miracles, status, or stages of attainment, the mind may remain dependent on recognition. A more useful question is whether one has become more aware, more able to take responsibility, and less likely to harm others in daily life.

“Chosen by heaven” or “having a special spiritual connection” is not an identity automatically granted by a birth chart. BaZi or religious language can describe a traditional view, but should not prove someone is more qualified, cannot err, or is exempt from ordinary responsibility. A profound experience can be valued while asking whether it is personal experience, cultural interpretation, or a fact others can verify. Not presenting a feeling as evidence does not erase its personal meaning.

Practice often begins with self-knowledge: noticing habitual reactions, triggering situations, and limits. This does not mean turning oneself into a fixed label. Ask whether the same thought recurs, how conflict is usually handled, and when one avoids or overcompensates. Self-mastery is not necessarily defeating another person. It can mean noticing a familiar reaction and not obeying it every time. When anger rises, avoid immediate harm; after failure, acknowledge the outcome and adjust. It can also mean admitting a need for rest or help.

Contentment is not giving up every wish. It is closer to distinguishing needs from endless comparison. People can pursue work and a better life without turning each gain or loss into a verdict on their worth. Daoist ideas of self-knowledge, self-mastery, and non-action can help consider how not to be led by every circumstance. Non-action need not mean doing nothing; it may mean avoiding force and excessive control in favor of a fitting response. This is a modern reading of classical thought, not the only ancient meaning.

Impermanence and dependent arising do not tell people to give up. Things change, and outcomes form through multiple conditions; because conditions can change, present action matters. Seeing impermanence can encourage care for what is here and timely attention to important matters, while preventing a temporary rise or fall from seeming eternal.

Karma should not become blame for another person’s suffering. Declaring that hardship is retribution from a past life lacks enough information and can harm the person again. A practical causal view asks how multiple conditions formed an event: personal choices, others’ actions, institutions, and chance may all participate. Multiple causes mean solutions need not be limited to blaming oneself or someone else.

A lamp parable about continuity and conditions can prompt reflection without imagining a cosmic system that keeps accounts for everyone. A parable opens understanding; it does not provide a measurable mechanism of the universe. Ask what sustains a habit and what support could change it. Shifting from abstract verdicts to concrete conditions makes practice more grounded.

Insight is not finished by seeing a thought once. Deep habits may return. Practice continues by recognizing old reactions, admitting their effects, and correcting them. Compassion does not mean tolerating every behavior; it includes honesty and reasonable boundaries. The image of a lotus in fire means trying to remain clear amid difficulty, not staying in harmful circumstances. Leaving an unsafe setting and seeking help may be wiser. Adversity does not automatically make people grow.

Short reflections often name six practices: acknowledge and correct a fault, keep compassion, understand conditions, release what is ready to end, practice steadily, and accept results. These are reminders rather than qualifications. Repentance without changed conduct is self-blame; compassion without boundaries can exhaust; letting go does not deny harm but stops a completed event from controlling today forever.

Practice also should not become a status contest about who meditates longer, knows more texts, or has unusual experiences. Whether a practice reduces attachment, deepens understanding, or improves relationships matters more than displaying terminology. Daoist, Buddhist, and Confucian traditions have distinct histories and should not be combined into a supposedly ancient universal teaching. A modern synthesis should be named as such.

Compassion and responsibility belong together. Understanding conditions does not excuse every action; naming harm does not deny the possibility of change. A victim need not prove exceptional goodness before deserving protection. Sometimes the fitting practice is ordinary: admit not knowing, apologize honestly, keep a promise, stop spreading unverified stories, or ask for help. Judge observable conduct rather than claims of attainment.

Clarity does not mean never being affected. People grieve, feel anger, become tired, and lose hope. Practice is not treating emotion as failure; it is knowing what one is experiencing, finding support, and not letting a passing feeling make every decision. Choose one small daily action—quiet minutes, noting a strong reaction, tidying a space, or reviewing a commitment. If you stop, begin again without turning the interruption into failure.

After the miracles are gone, practice remains: self-knowledge, self-mastery, contentment, choice after emotion, understanding causes, and reducing harm in daily life. Keeping one clear place within a noisy world is not escape; it leaves room for judgment. BaZi may offer an aid for examining a traditional structure, but practice remains in ordinary life, not in a special identity granted by someone else.
